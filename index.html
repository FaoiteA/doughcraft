    Baking Basics

    Tips, guides and techniques to help you bake with confidence.

    Understanding Dough

    The science behind great bread.

    What is dough, really?

    Dough is a network of gluten strands trapping gas from fermentation. Get those two things right — gluten and gas — and everything else falls into place.

    The four stages
    • Mix — hydrate the flour, activate the yeast.
    • Ferment — yeast eats sugars, produces CO₂ and flavour compounds.
    • Shape — build surface tension so the dough holds structure.
    • Bake — set the structure and develop crust colour.
    Why temperature matters

    Yeast is a living thing. At 26°C fermentation runs fast; at 18°C it slows to a crawl. A warm kitchen means less waiting; a cool one means more flavour development.

    Flour Guide

    Find the right flour for your bake.

    Protein is everything

    Protein content in flour determines gluten strength. More protein means chewier, structured breads. Less means tender, cake-like results.

    • "00" flour (11–12%) — silky, elastic. The Neapolitan pizza standard. Look for Caputo or Odlums 00 in larger Tescos and Italian delis.
    • Strong White Flour (12–14%) — this is "bread flour". Odlums Strong White is the Irish standard, in every supermarket. Great oven spring for artisan loaves.
    • Plain Flour (9–11%) — versatile everyday flour. Fine for enriched doughs and some quick pizzas but weaker gluten.
    • Wholemeal Flour (13–14%) — nutty, denser, absorbs more water. Odlums Strong Wholemeal is easy to find.
    Yeast in Ireland

    The most common in Irish supermarkets is Fast Action Dried Yeast (McDougalls, Tesco own brand, Doves Farm Quick). This is "instant yeast" — add it straight to the flour, no blooming needed.

    If you have Dried Active Yeast (Allinson's) instead, bloom it first: dissolve in warm water with a pinch of sugar, wait 5–10 minutes until foamy, then proceed.

    A rule of thumb

    Swapping flours changes hydration needs. Wholemeal drinks more water than white. Start 5% higher on hydration when using it.

    Shaping Techniques

    Learn how to shape like a pro.

    Why shape matters

    Shaping builds surface tension. That tension is what holds the dough's structure during proof and gives you a tall, open crumb instead of a flat pancake.

    Three core shapes
    • Boule — round loaf. Gently pull the dough toward you in a circle, rotating as you go. Aim for a taut, smooth top.
    • Batard — oval loaf. Fold like an envelope, then roll gently into a log.
    • Pizza ball — smooth top, pinched bottom. Cup the dough in your palm and drag on the counter to tighten.
    Handle gently

    You spent hours building air into the dough during bulk. Don't punch it out. Shape with a light hand.

    Troubleshooting

    Fix common dough problems.

    Dense, gummy crumb

    Under-fermented or under-baked. Give bulk more time, or bake 5–10 minutes longer. Check internal temperature reaches 96°C.

    Flat, no oven spring

    Over-proofed. Dough was pushed too far during final proof. Shorten proof next time; poke test — dough should spring back slowly, not stay dented.

    Pale crust

    Oven wasn't hot enough or humidity was too high. Preheat longer and remove any Dutch oven lid earlier.

    Dough won't rise

    Dead yeast, or the water was too hot and killed it. Water should feel just warm — never hot. Test yeast in warm water with a pinch of sugar; if it doesn't foam in 10 minutes, buy fresh yeast.

    Sticky, unworkable dough

    Too much water for the flour. Add a small amount of flour and knead in. Or embrace it — high-hydration doughs give great open crumb.

    Ingredients Around the World

    What the ingredients are called where you live.

    DoughCraft recipes use Irish/UK supermarket names. If you're baking elsewhere, here's what to look for.

    Flour
    • Strong White Flour (Ireland/UK) = bread flour (US, Canada, Australia); plain strong flour; farine T65 (France); Type 550 (Germany); farina tipo 0 (Italy). Look for ~12–14% protein.
    • "00" Flour — same name worldwide. Finely milled Italian pizza and pasta flour. Caputo is the classic brand.
    • Wholemeal Flour (Ireland/UK) = whole wheat flour (US, Canada, Australia); farine complète (France); Vollkornmehl (Germany); farina integrale (Italy).
    • Plain Flour (Ireland/UK) = all-purpose flour (US, Canada, Australia); farine T45 (France); Type 405 (Germany). Not ideal for artisan breads but works for enriched doughs.
    Yeast
    • Fast Action Dried Yeast (Ireland/UK) = instant yeast, rapid rise yeast, quick yeast (US, Canada); levure instantanée (France); Instant Trockenhefe (Germany). Add straight to flour — no blooming needed.
    • Dried Active Yeast (Ireland/UK) = active dry yeast (US, Canada, Australia); levure sèche (France); Trockenhefe (Germany). Must bloom first: dissolve in warm water with a pinch of sugar, wait 5–10 min until foamy, then use.
    • Fresh Yeast (cake yeast, compressed yeast) — use roughly 3× the weight of dried. So 2g dried = ~6g fresh.
    Salt
    • Fine sea salt or table salt — what all DoughCraft recipes assume by default (measured in grams).
    • Flaky salt (Maldon, Kosher salt in US) — larger crystals for finishing, e.g. topping focaccia. Not a 1:1 substitute for fine salt by weight.
    Sourdough starter

    Called starter, levain, mother, chef, madre, or desem depending on where you learned to bake. All the same thing — a live culture of wild yeast and bacteria.

    To get one: ask a local artisan bakery (many give away or sell a scoop cheap), buy dried starter online (Bakery Bits, Sourdoughs International), or make your own from scratch over 5–7 days with flour and water.

    A note on measuring

    All DoughCraft recipes use grams for accuracy. If you're used to cups, get a digital kitchen scale — it costs €10 and completely changes bread baking. Cup measurements vary massively depending on how you scoop.
